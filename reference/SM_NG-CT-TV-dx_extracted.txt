[Heading 1] Zimbabwe’s national VDS treatment guidelines
[normal] Figure S1 reproduces Zimbabwe’s national guidelines for the management of vaginal discharge syndrome (VDS). According to these guidelines, women should be given a risk assessment to determine whether treatment for NG and CT is indicated, ideally with the addition of a genital examination with/without speculum depending on patient preferences. Particular factors that should indicate treatment for NG and CT include having a partner who has reported discharge or ulcers; being under 25 years; being unmarried; or having a new/more than one sexual partner in the past three months. Even if a risk assessment is negative, however, treatment for NG+CT should be given if symptoms are considered to be consistent with cervical infection, e.g. profuse/offensive yellow, green or pus-like discharge.



[Heading 1] HIV model 
[normal] In addition to modeling the three curable STIs contributing to VDS, we model HIV transmission, progression, and treatment. We include HIV for two reasons: 1) to leverage the relatively robust data on HIV prevalence, incidence, mortality, and treatment over time to improve calibration of the underlying sexual network; 2) to capture the effects of HIV in shaping the current and future epidemiology of STIs in Zimbabwe, given the well-established interdependence between these infections. Although not yet clearly demonstrated in practice (16–21), there is some evidence that improved management of STIs may lead to reductions in HIV incidence (17). 

[normal] Several of the authors of this paper have previously published documented, tested, and validated models of HIV, including the Optima HIV model (22–25) and the EMOD-HIV model (26,27). To develop the model of HIV used in this paper, we drew heavily upon our previously published work to inform parameter values, model structure, and other assumptions.

[Heading 2] Natural history
[Heading 3] Untreated infection
[Heading 4] Acute and latent infection
[normal] Within our model, we use CD4 counts as the key metric for determining an individual's transmissibility and health outcomes. Prior to infection, all agents begin with a baseline level of CD4 cells. There is evidence of notable variation in baseline CD4 counts for HIV-negative individuals both between individuals and across geographies (28). We assume that the baseline level is normally distributed with a mean of 800 and standard deviation of 50 in line with available studies of similar populations (29–31). Upon an infection, individuals enter an initial acute stage of infection, characterized by rapidly expanding viral load and falling CD4 counts (32). This initial acute phase is estimated to last 2.90 (1.23-6.00) months (33); we represent this by drawing the distribution of acute infection from a log-normal distribution with mean of 3 months and standard deviation of 1 month. During this phase, we assume that CD4 levels decline linearly to reach an initial latent value cd4_latent ~ N(500, 50). 

[normal] Latent infection is a chronic and largely symptomless phase of infection, during which CD4 counts remain relatively stable or gradually declining. The CASCADE study followed ~18,500 individuals and found that the median time from seroconversion to CD4 of 500 was 1.19 (1.12–1.26) years, 4.19 (4.09–4.28) years to CD4 of 350, and 7.93 (7.76–8.09) years until CD4 of 200. In our model, we simplify this process by drawing an overall duration of latent infection from a log-normal distribution with a mean of 12 years and standard deviation of 3 years. During this time, we assume that CD4 levels decline linearly from 500 to 200. 
[Heading 4] Late-stage infection, AIDS, and AIDS-related death
[normal] After this time, CD4 counts begin to decline more rapidly as individuals enter late-stage infection. We again assume that individual CD4 counts decline linearly over this phase, beginning from the level at the conclusion of their latent phase and declining down to zero (corresponding to AIDS-related death) over a period of time which we take to be log-normally distributed with a mean of  3 years, in line with the parameter values used in the Optima HIV model (23).

[Heading 4] Viral dynamics
[normal] Virally suppressive ART has been shown to be highly effective at preventing transmission of HIV, with an estimated 93% reduction in linked transmissions in the pivotal Cohen study (37). Other studies have estimated even greater reductions, finding either no or very few instances of transmission in serodiscordant couples with a virally suppressed partner (41,42). Because our model does not differentiate between those who are virally suppressed vs not, we assume that all individuals on ART are 92.5% less transmissible, which roughly corresponds to 90% of individuals being virally suppressed (and therefore 96% less transmissible taking the average of results across partner studies (37,41,42)), and the remaining 10% not being virally suppressed (and therefore 50% less transmissible following the Optima HIV model assumptions (34)). 

[normal] We assume that ART takes 6 months before reaching full efficacy in preventing transmission, and over the first 6 months of treatment, we assume that relative transmissibility declines linearly until it reaches 7.5% of its untreated level after 6 months. If an individual stops ART, we assume that their viral load immediately rebounds to its untreated levels. 

[normal] We also assume increases in relative transmissibility during both the early acute and the late stages of infection. Following the approach used in the Optima HIV model, we set individuals in acute-stage infection to be 5.6x more infectious (43) and individuals in late-stage infection to be 7.2x more infectious compared to people in the latent phase of infection (34).

[Heading 2] Transmission 
[normal] For sexual transmission, we use an estimated per-act transmission probability of 0.04% (0.01%-0.14%) for female-to-male transmission and 0.08% (0.06%-0.11%) for male-to-female transmission, following the Optima HIV model which was based on a 2009 systematic review and meta-analysis of heterosexual risk of HIV transmission (34,44). For maternal transmission, we assume an overall transmission risk of 36.7% for breastfeeding mothers and 20.5% (14.0%-27.0%) for non-breastfeeding mothers, again following the Optima HIV model which was based on a randomized clinical trial of 400 women (45). 

[Heading 2] Care-seeking, testing, and treatment
[normal] Data on HIV testing rates are available from the DHS and from the Zimbabwe PHIA study (46,47). We capture four different modes of HIV testing: symptomatic testing rates for those with AIDS (CD4<200), testing rates among female sex workers (FSW), testing rates at antenatal clinics (ANC), and other general population testing rates. Values for all of these are adjusted to match the proportion of people living with HIV who are aware of their status, reported as 86.8% in ZIMPHIA. In 2020, this implies annual testing rates of 85% among people with AIDS, 75% among FSW, 90% among ANCs, and 10% among the general population.

[normal] Across populations, there is significant variation in health-seeking behavior and preferences. We do not attempt to model this in a detailed or causative way, but we do assign each individual agent in the model a care-seeking propensity, drawn from a normal distribution centered at 1, with higher values representing individuals who are more likely to seek care. We then use this care-seeking value to help determine which individuals are more likely to commence treatment. Within the model, any individual who tests positive is eligible to commence ART. We do not explicitly capture the CD4 eligibility constraints that were in effect for much of the early era of treatment, but rather we use an ART prioritization algorithm that accounts for both CD4 count and care-seeking behavior, as follows: 
[normal] Those who have just tested positive to HIV are assumed to be initiated onto ART, with an assumed initiation rate of 90%
[normal] We then determine the difference between the modeled estimate of the number of people receiving treatment and data from UNAIDS on the number of people receiving ART each year from 1990 to 2023.
[normal] If this difference is negative, it implies that there is additional available treatment to be distributed, e.g. to people who tested positive at previous points in time but did not immediately initiate treatment, or to people who stopped treatment. Each diagnosed, untreated individual is given a ranking defined as CD4/care-seeking. Individuals are then given treatment starting with those with the lowest values. This implies, for example, that an individual with a low CD4 count of 200 and low care-seeking of 0.5 would be equally likely to commence treatment compared to an individual with a CD4 count of 400 and care-seeking of 1. 
[normal] If this difference is positive, it implies that the model has overestimated the number of people on treatment. We therefore remove some people. Each treated individual is given a ranking defined as CD4/care-seeking. Individual agents are then removed from treatment starting with those with the highest values, since this corresponds to people with either high CD4 counts or low care-seeking behavior.

[normal] Upon commencing ART, we determine the length of time that a person is likely to continue ART. By default, we assume long durations of treatment (log-normally distributed with a mean of 20 years), since the algorithm above will naturally impose treatment interruptions especially for those with low care-seeking behavior. 

[Heading 1] Interactions between HIV and discharging STIs
[normal] People living with HIV (PLHIV) have elevated prevalence of STIs. A systematic review of 37 studies of STI-HIV co-infection prevalence estimated the mean point-prevalence of STI infection among PLHIV as 16.3%, with syphilis, NG, CT, and TV being the most common (48). The elevated prevalence is reflective of both behavioral and biological interactions between STIs and HIV. The behavioral interactions will be implicitly accounted for within our modeling framework because there is a common sexual network responsible for all transmission events, so those with riskier sexual behavior will already be at elevated risk of both HIV and STI transmission. In terms of the biological interactions, there are numerous studies pointing to evidence that STIs can increase the risks of both transmission and acquisition of HIV (44,49–53). Table S4 reports the parameters used within the model to capture the relative risks of HIV acquisition and transmission by STI and by sex. These are generally taken as the midpoints of ranges reported across systematic reviews/meta-analyses and large prospective cohort studies comparing HIV risk for those with and without an STI by sex, as referenced in the caption Table S3.

[Heading 2] Age and risk categories
[normal] We use an age- and risk-structured sexual network where agents are assigned into one of 3 main risk groups, as summarized in Table S5. 

[normal] Table S5: sexual risk group definitions

[normal] On top of these risk groups, a proportion of each of the groups above engages in sex work. We assume that 5% of women will engage in sex work at some point in their lives, and that 20% of men will be clients of sex workers at some point. These values are derived based on an estimated point prevalence of FSW of 1.25%, and assuming that the average duration of sex work is around 4 years (55).

[normal] Several of the key network parameters also vary by age: we divide females into three major age bins: teens (<20), young adults (20-25) and adults (25+).

[Heading 2] Pair formation
[normal] On each time step, we match new pairs as follows:
[normal] Find agents who are sexually active (i.e. past their age of debut) and have fewer than their desired number of partners (as determined by concurrency parameters). These agents are eligible to look for partners. Some proportion of eligible women (p_pair_form) are assumed to be looking for a partner. This parameter value is estimated as part of model calibration. We use this number to determine how many matches to make.
[normal] For each female agent seeking a new partner, we determine the preferred age of her partner using the age difference parameters given in Table S8. We then match the males to the females by sorting the male ages and matching them to the desired female ages. 
[normal] If both partners are in the same risk group, we use a parameter p_matched_stable to determine the share that will form stable relationships. The duration of the stable relationship varies depending on the risk group and the age of the agents (stable_dur_pars). If they have not formed a stable match, they form a casual relationship whose duration also depends on age and risk group (casual_dur_pars). These parameters are all documented in Table S9.
[normal] If partners are in different risk groups, we use another parameter p_mismatched_casual to determine the share that will form casual relationships (whose duration depends on age and risk group, casual_dur_pars). If they do not form a casual relationship, they form an instantaneous relationship that dissolves on the next time step. 

[normal] Table S8: distribution of the preferred age differences between females in each age/risk group and their partners

[normal] Table S9: probabilities and durations of stable and casual matches

[normal] We then match new sex work pairs separately, as follows:
[normal] Determine the proportion of clients seeking a sex worker. The annual rate at which clients seek FSWs is assumed to be 6, equivalent to 1 new SW partner every 2 months.
[normal] Randomly assign a sex worker to each client (age preferences are not used here). Sex workers are allowed to have more than one client.

[Heading 2] Behavior within pairs
[normal] When pairs form, we determine their annual number of acts (drawn from a log-normal distribution with mean of 90 and standard deviation of 30) and their propensity for condom use. Rates of condom use vary over time and by risk group.

[Heading 2] Pair dissolution
[normal] Pairs are dissolved at the end of their durations. We do not enforce any lag between ending one relationship and commencing another, but the parameter p_pair_form moderates how quickly females will re-enter relationships. 


[Heading 1] Model calibration
[normal] We split the calibration process into two phases.

[normal] The first phase focuses on the sexual network by adjusting parameters of the network in order to model HIV transmission, progression, and treatment, fitting to data on the overall population size, the number of people living with HIV (PLHIV), HIV prevalence, the number of new HIV infections, and the number of HIV-related deaths.from UNAIDS over 1990-2025 whilst simultaneously aiming for near-alignment with sexual behavior data from Zimbabwe’s DHS (age at first sex, lifetime numbers of sexual partners, and marriage rates by age). We did not treat the sexual behavior data as a fixed calibration target due to common mis-reporting issues with these data. In this first step, we use placeholder estimates of population-level STI prevalence and fit the parameters of the sexual network by minimizing the difference between the model estimates of HIV outcomes and data from the UN WPP and UNAIDS. Table S10 summarizes the parameters that we adjust, the prior ranges of these parameters, and the final parameter values identified by calibration. Figure S4 we show the model fit to the HIV data.

[normal] Building on this calibrated sexual network, the second phase estimated parameters for STI transmissibility, symptomaticity, and care-seeking separately for each of the three implementation scenarios. We varied 7 key parameters in each calibration: the symptomatic share of infections in women for NG (10-20%), CT (10-30%), and TV (15-75%), the proportion of women experiencing VDS who seek clinical care (25-75%), and the male-to-female per-act transmission probability of NG, CT, and TV (2-30%). We fit to STI data on the prevalence of NG, CT, and TV in young women and pregnant women over 1990-2025 (56–58).  Table S11 shows the prior and posterior ranges for the parameters adjusted during this step. By tuning these parameters for each scenario, we can estimate the proportion of women treated consistent with historical prevalence estimates.

[normal] For each calibration, we run 10,000 trials selected using a Bayesian optimization algorithm called Optuna (59), a Python package integrated into STIsim for the purpose of finding the parameters that minimize the normalized sum of absolute differences between the model and the data for each metric and at each timepoint over the period from 1990-2025. 

[normal] Throughout the paper, we report estimates derived from the 500 best-fitting parameter sets.
